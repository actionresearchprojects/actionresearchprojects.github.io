    ARC Cool Buildings Standard¶

    Thermal comfort and building performance in hot, and hot and humid, climates.

    Browse the pages below, or ask a question.

    ARC Technical Criteria¶

    Overview¶

    • Climate and weather - Why performance is assessed against the current climate rather than a fixed benchmark, and why benchmarks themselves keep moving.
    • How ARC assesses building performance - How ARC judges a building: monitored environmental data against a humidity-modified adaptive comfort model, by climate category.
    • Introduction - What this document is, who it is for, and ARC's aim of a building standard for hot and hot-humid climates where Passivhaus in one step is not realistic.
    • Success criteria for building modelling and performance - What the ARC Cool Buildings Standard sets out to achieve: year-round thermal comfort through passive design, while mitigating danger during heatwaves.
    • The ARC framework - two Steps and three Zones - The shape of the framework - two strategic Steps, then three Zones - and what it is designed to support.
    • What influences thermal comfort - The personal and environmental factors comfort depends on, and why a single universal building standard across climates and cultures is so hard.
    • What the Cool Buildings Programme provides - The five parts of the programme: design strategies, the comfort model, building science resources, prescriptive measures and exemplar buildings.

    Patterns¶

    • Designing a heat refuge - The passive measures that keep a heat refuge below its limit when the power is off: orientation, shading, reflective surfaces, heat-shedding roofs and window openings.
    • Leveraging adaptive comfort - the three-zone strategy - Using the three-zone approach to arrange a building's form and layout, so it can still operate when the electricity supply is interrupted.
    • Step 1 and Step 2 - Step 1 is a naturally ventilated building that can be retrofitted over time; Step 2 is fully mechanically conditioned but still able to run on natural ventilation.
    • Zone 1 - mechanically cooled and ventilated - What a mechanically cooled and ventilated space must do, including ventilation when air conditioning is running.
    • Zone 2 - the communal heat refuge - The communal heat refuge: a space large enough for all occupants, usable during extreme heatwaves.
    • Zone 3 - transient and buffer spaces - Corridors, washrooms and other spaces people pass through, which may run hotter than zones 1 and 2 and can shade them.

    Reference¶

    • Defining and monitoring heatwaves - How heatwaves are defined and monitored internationally, and why the global south is poorly served by the existing arrangements.
    • Defining heatwaves and the heat index - How heatwaves are defined, and why organisations increasingly express hot weather as a feels-like value or heat index.
    • Heatwaves, power cuts and operational resilience - Why heatwaves and power cuts arrive together, and what designing for operational resilience means in practice.
    • Measuring thermal comfort - the temperatures - Dry bulb, wet bulb and dew point temperature, and the heat index - what each measures and when it matters.
    • Meeting everyday comfort needs - What claiming performance to the ARC standard requires: at least twelve months of measured data, and which comfort model applies where.
    • Physiological adaptation to heat - How quickly people adapt to outdoor conditions, why a running mean beats a monthly average, and what has to be measured.
    • R&D brief - air conditioning and ventilation for hot humid climates - The brief for research into air conditioning and ventilation suited to hot humid climates, and the constraints it has to work within.
    • Resources - Where ARC's supporting material and tools can be found.
    • Success criteria - the temperature thresholds - The temperature thresholds a monitored zone 1 or 2 space must meet, and the separate limits that apply during a heatwave.
    • Temperatures of transient spaces during heatwaves - The limits for spaces people use for more than an hour a day, in hot dry and hot humid climates.
    • The humidity-inclusive comfort model - The model behind the thresholds, why more humid climates need more stringent standards, and why operative temperature is the correct metric.
    • Thermal comfort models - The models used to judge comfort, and the factors they take account of: clothing, activity, humidity and air speed.
