    ARC Cool Buildings Standard¶

    Thermal comfort and building performance in hot, and hot and humid, climates.

    Draft — imported, not yet reviewed

    These pages come from the ARC Technical Criteria document of 16 April 2026. They have not yet been reviewed page by page in this format, and several temperature thresholds are still being settled. Those are flagged on the pages concerned.

    Browse the pages below, or ask a question.

    Overview¶

    • Climate and weather — Building performance is normally reported against the 'current climate', but that baseline is itself moving. Performance now has to be assessed against both current and predicted future extreme weather.
    • Introduction to the ARC Cool Buildings Standard — What this guidance is, who it is for, and why ARC has developed a building performance standard for hot and hot-humid climates rather than adopting an existing one.
    • Success criteria for building modelling and performance — The ARC Cool Buildings Standard aims for all-year-round thermal comfort in hot and hot-humid climates, principally through passive design, while mitigating dangerous conditions during heatwaves.
    • The ARC framework — two Steps and three Zones — The framework has two Steps, which set the building's overall servicing strategy, and three Zones, which arrange its form and layout. Together they support incremental improvement over time.
    • The Cool Buildings Programme — The five components of ARC's programme — design strategies, the dynamic adaptive-comfort model, building science resources, prescriptive measures and exemplar buildings.
    • What influences thermal comfort — Thermal comfort depends on six factors — four environmental and two personal — plus acclimatisation, expectation and a person's thermal history. Air temperature alone predicts comfort badly.

    Patterns¶

    • Designing a heat refuge — The passive measures that keep a heat refuge below its temperature limit when the power is off — orientation, shading, reflective surfaces, heat-shedding roofs, window openings and draughtproofing.
    • Step 1 and Step 2 — Step 1 is a naturally ventilated building designed so mechanical cooling can be added later without locking out passive operation. Step 2 is a fully conditioned building that can still run naturally ventilated.
    • The three-zone strategy — Zones arrange a building's form and layout around its context, wind direction and the sun's path, so that it can still operate passively when the power fails.
    • Zone 1 — mechanically cooled and ventilated — Zone 1 spaces run air conditioning efficiently and, in less harsh climates, can switch off and work naturally ventilated. Air conditioning without adequate room ventilation is strongly discouraged.
    • Zone 2 — the communal heat refuge — A room or zone large enough for all building occupants, designed to stay comfortable during heatwaves using natural ventilation and adaptive comfort measures — and to keep working during a power cut.
    • Zone 3 — transient and buffer spaces — Corridors, washrooms, stores and showers. Because people pass through rather than stay, these can run hotter than Zones 1 and 2 — and can be placed to shade the rooms behind them.

    Reference¶

    • Defining and monitoring heatwaves — How the WMO, the Met Office and the IPCC define a heatwave, the threshold methods in use, who monitors them, and why coverage gaps matter for the global south.
    • Measuring thermal comfort — the temperatures — Dry bulb, wet bulb and dew point temperature explained, plus the heat index. These are the measurements the ARC comfort model and its thresholds are built on.
    • Physiological adaptation to heat — People adapt to the weather they have recently experienced. ARC prefers the running mean outdoor temperature over a monthly average, because adaptation drops off sharply after about seven days.
    • Practical advice during a heatwave — What occupants can actually do when it is dangerously hot — siestas, moving around the building, tepid showers, fans, when to open and close windows, and a solar-backed shelter.
    • R&D brief — air conditioning and ventilation for hot humid climates — ARC's technical brief for a prototype air-conditioning unit that balances cooling against dehumidification and supplies fresh air without a large energy penalty.
    • Research resources and databases — The open datasets and published research underpinning ARC's comfort model, including the ASHRAE global database of thermal comfort field measurements.
    • Success criteria — the temperature thresholds — Zone 1 and 2 spaces should stay below the comfort model's humidity-adjusted thresholds for at least 90% of the year. Separate, higher limits apply during heatwaves.
    • Temperatures in transient spaces during heatwaves — Kitchens, bathrooms and corridors are used for more than an hour a day and must not exceed human limits — 40 °C air temperature in hot dry climates, 32–34 °C wet bulb in hot humid climates.
    • The ARC comfort model — ARC uses a humidity-inclusive adaptive comfort model. More humid climates require more stringent comfort standards, because the body's ability to sweat is diminished in humid air.
    • Thermal comfort models and their inputs — The two model families — steady-state heat balance and adaptive feedback — and the four inputs that drive them: clothing, activity, humidity and air speed.
